Assignment: describe a morpho-syntactic phenomenon

This is an assignment for students of KOL/MORS to deepen their understanding. You should work in pairs (or if there are an uneven number of students, one group of one or three). Each group submits one paper. Include a short contribution statement at the end of the paper (after the conclusion, before the references) saying who did what.

Your task is to describe an interesting linguistic phenomenon. Interesting is subjective, you need to motivate why you think it is interesting. If you are not sure discuss it with me. Your paper should include

  • Examples of the phenomenon, glossed if not in English
  • References to the linguistic literature (at least 3 papers)
  • A description of the phenomenon that allows us to understand what it is

Ideally you should also include one or more of the following

  • A formal analysis of part of the problem
  • A survey of speakers' grammaticality judgements

The final paper should be in LaTeX, eight to twelve pages single column (use the article class, 12pt) + references. You should submit PDF via moodle, don't forget to include your names!

Deadline: see the course schedule. The date is kept there, and only there, so that it cannot drift out of step with this page.

Weight: 20%, made up of 14 for the paper and 2 for each of the three milestones below. This is the extra task that makes KOL/MORS worth six credits rather than five, so MORS is marked out of 120 and then scaled by 5/6. In terms of your final mark the assignment is therefore worth about 17%. See the assessment components on the course page.

Milestones. Do not leave this to January. There are three short submissions during the semester, in weeks 4, 6 and 9, each worth 2 marks, given for handing in something sensible rather than for how polished it is. They are due with that week's problem set — 23:59 the following Monday — and are marked ⓵ ⓶ ⓷ in the last column of the course schedule.

  1. ⓵ Topic and partner. One paragraph: which phenomenon, why you think it is interesting, and who you are working with. This is also when I can tell you if it is too big.
  2. ⓶ 3 references. A .bib file with your three references, and two or three sentences on each saying what it argues. Hand this in as a PDF built from the template, with the citations coming out properly — that way you sort LaTeX out in November, while you can still ask me in class, rather than the night before the deadline.
  3. ⓷ Data and introduction. Your examples, glossed, and the first page of the paper. Glossing is the fiddliest part of the LaTeX, so it is worth hitting it while there are still classes left.

Start from the template. It has the sections you need, the packages we recommend, and worked examples of numbered examples, glosses, trees and feature structures.

Some sample phenomena

You can take these, or come up with your own, but run it past me first.

Czech Morphological Paradigms for Nouns

Describe the most common paradigms, and note which forms are under-specified. Make a type hierarchy of cases that has nodes for all relevant cases. For example, for feminine nouns ending in a (such as žena), ženy could be gen.sg, nom.pl, acc.pl or voc.pl; ženě can be dat.sg or loc.sg, while žena is only nom.sg. So there must be at least nodes that cover gen.sg+nom.pl+acc.pl, one that covers dat.sg+loc.sg and one that covers only nom.sg, ...

Czech vs English Adverb vs Adjective

Czech will often use an adverb where English uses an adjective

  1. Venku je horko --- It is hot outside
  2. Kateřina se tvářila naštvaně --- Katerina looked angry

What kind of contexts are these? Can you describe them precisely?

Czech second position

Czech clitics must go in the second position. What do you do if there are two of them?

  1. * Snažím se se učit. "I am trying to learn"

There may be multiple strategies. This would be a good choice if you like to survey people, ...

Czech numerals and agreement

From five upwards, a Czech numeral takes the genitive plural, and the verb shows default neuter singular rather than agreeing with the noun.

  1. Čtyři ženy přišly --- Four women came
  2. Pět žen přišlo --- Five women came

Where does the agreement come from, and which element is the head? Does the same pattern hold in the other cases, and with the numerals tisíc, milion, or pár? Good for the formal analysis option.

Animacy in the Czech masculine declension

For masculine animate nouns the accusative looks like the genitive; for inanimates it looks like the nominative.

  1. Vidím psa --- I see the dog (acc = gen)
  2. Vidím hrad --- I see the castle (acc = nom)

Which nouns count as animate, and what do you do with the ones that waver (robot, sněhulák, names of teams, names of viruses)? This extends the paradigm question above, so a second pair could take it. Good for the formal analysis option.

Multiple wh-fronting

English fronts one wh-phrase; Czech fronts all of them.

  1. Kdo co komu dal? --- Who gave what to whom?

Which orders are possible, and which are not? What does this say about the filler-gap mechanism we develop for long distance dependencies? Good for the formal analysis option.

Negative concord

Czech requires negation on the verb even when the clause already has a negative pronoun.

  1. Nikdo nic neřekl --- Nobody said anything
  2. (literally: nobody nothing not-said)

What licenses what? Is the negative marking on the verb the same thing as the negation in nikdo, or something else? Good for the formal analysis option.

Czech reflexive se and si

The same forms turn up as reflexives, as reciprocals, on inherently reflexive verbs, and in impersonal passives.

Is this one lexical item or several? This overlaps with the second position question above, so one pair could take the clitic placement and another the lexical entries. Good for either option.

Agreement with coordinated subjects

When two nouns are coordinated, something has to decide the gender and number of the verb.

  1. Petr a Marie přišli --- Petr and Marie came (masculine animate plural)

What happens with two inanimates, with mixed animacy, or when the nearest conjunct disagrees with the resolved form? Speakers do not all agree, which is what makes this worth asking them. Good for the speaker survey option.

Aspect and prefixation

Czech prefixes change aspect, and sometimes more than aspect.

  1. psát --- to write (imperfective)
  2. napsat, přepsat, podepsat --- to write, to rewrite, to sign

Which part of this is morphology and which is syntax? Do any of the prefixes change the valency of the verb, and if so, how would you record that in the lexicon? Can you stack prefixes?

Czech possessive adjectives

Czech derives an adjective from a noun to express possession: matka gives matčin, otec gives otcův.

The result is an adjective, but it still has a possessor --- and that possessor cannot be modified. There is no good way to say "my mother's book" using matčin. Why not? What does that tell you about where the derivation happens? Good for the formal analysis option.

Czech diminutives

pes gives psík and pejsek, and the process can apply more than once.

How productive is it, what does it attach to, and what does it actually mean? It is not simply "small".

The comparative correlative

Neither half of this is a well-formed clause on its own, and yet the whole is fine.

  1. The more you read, the better you write
  2. * You read the more

What is the construction, and what are its parts? A good fit for Sign-Based Construction Grammar, and it does not need Czech.

Classifiers

Many languages require a classifier between a numeral and a noun: Mandarin sān běn shū "three (volume) books", Japanese hon san-satsu "books three-(volume)".

What determines which classifier a noun takes? What happens with loanwords and new coinages? A good choice if you speak one of these languages.
